**Language:** English. **Level:** first or second year of an LLB, introductory EU law.

## Unit A. Free movement of goods (Articles 34 to 36 TFEU)

### Content
This unit covers the prohibition of quantitative restrictions and measures having equivalent effect (MEQRs) on imports and exports between Member States, and how that prohibition developed in the case law:
- from the broad Dassonville formula;
- through mutual recognition and mandatory requirements (Cassis de Dijon);
- to the selling-arrangements exception (Keck);
- to the market-access test (Commission v Italy, trailers; Ker-Optika).

It also covers the grounds of justification in Article 36 TFEU and the proportionality review.

### Learning objectives
At the end of the unit the student is able to:

- **A1.** State the prohibitions in Articles 34 and 35 TFEU and the derogation grounds in Article 36 TFEU. Explain the old numbering: Articles 30 to 36 EEC, then 28 to 30 EC, now 34 to 36 TFEU.
- **A2.** Reproduce the Dassonville definition of an MEQR (Case 8/74, para 5) and explain why it is broad: "directly or indirectly, actually or potentially".
- **A3.** Explain mutual recognition and the "mandatory requirements" in Cassis de Dijon (Case 120/78, paras 8 and 14). Distinguish them from the Article 36 TFEU grounds.
- **A4.** Explain the Keck distinction between product requirements and certain selling arrangements (Joined Cases C-267/91 and C-268/91, paras 15 to 17). Apply its two conditions:
  - the rules apply to all relevant traders;
  - they affect domestic and imported products in the same manner, in law and in fact.

  The student can also explain precisely what Keck changed. Keck says "contrary to what has previously been decided" (para 16): it limits how the Dassonville formula applies to such selling arrangements. It does not abolish the formula.
- **A5.** Set out the three-part summary in Commission v Italy (C-110/05, para 37), including the "market access" limb. Apply it to rules on how a product may be used (paras 56 to 58).
- **A6.** Apply Keck to a modern sales channel, using Ker-Optika (C-108/09, paras 45 to 56). The national ban on internet sales of contact lenses is a selling arrangement. It fails the second Keck condition, so it is an MEQR.
- **A7.** Assess a justification for appropriateness and necessity, using:
  - road safety in Commission v Italy (paras 59 to 69, where the measure is justified);
  - public health in Ker-Optika (paras 57 to 75, where the measure goes beyond what is necessary).
- **A8.** Solve a problem question step by step:
  1. Is the measure an MEQR?
  2. Is it a product requirement or a selling arrangement, or a use restriction?
  3. Is there a justification?
  4. Is the measure proportionate?

  Cite the ECLI of each case relied on.

## Unit B. Direct effect and primacy of EU law

### Content
This unit covers the founding constitutional principles of the EU legal order:
- direct effect (Van Gend en Loos);
- primacy (Costa v ENEL);
- the national court's duty to set aside conflicting national law (Simmenthal);
- the limit that directives have no horizontal direct effect (Marshall).

It also covers the preliminary reference procedure (Article 267 TFEU) through which these principles were developed, and the legal effect of directives (Article 288 TFEU).

### Learning objectives
At the end of the unit the student is able to:

- **B1.** Explain why Van Gend en Loos (Case 26/62) described the Community as "a new legal order of international law". State the conditions for a Treaty provision to have direct effect. The English text has no paragraph numbers, so the student cites it by ECLI and ECR page.
- **B2.** Explain the reasoning in Costa v ENEL (Case 6/64): the Treaty created "its own legal system" that cannot "be overridden by domestic legal provisions, however framed". The text has no paragraph numbers.
- **B3.** State the national court's duty in Simmenthal (Case 106/77, paras 21 and 24). In the words of para 24, a national court must "give full effect to those provisions, if necessary refusing of its own motion to apply any conflicting provision of national legislation, even if adopted subsequently", and it is "not necessary for the court to request or await the prior setting aside of such provision by legislative or other constitutional means".
- **B4.** Explain why a directive "may not of itself impose obligations on an individual" (Marshall, Case 152/84, para 48). Explain why a directive can still be relied on against the State acting as employer (para 49).
- **B5.** Describe the function of Article 267 TFEU and the effect of directives under Article 288 TFEU. Link each to the cases above.

## Assessment-style activities in the demo (CF features)
- Tutor questions answered from the files in `sources/` only, with the ECLI and paragraph shown.
- Trap questions, for example:
  - "Did Keck overrule Dassonville?"
  - "Is Article 30 the justification article?" The answer depends on the numbering. Article 30 EEC (used in Dassonville, Cassis and Keck) was the import prohibition. After the Treaty of Amsterdam renumbering, Article 28 EC was the prohibition and Article 30 EC the justification (see Commission v Italy, C-110/05, paras 34 and 59, which use that numbering). The current provisions are Article 34 TFEU (prohibition, "ex Article 28 TEC") and Article 36 TFEU (justification, "ex Article 30 TEC"), as printed in the TFEU text.
