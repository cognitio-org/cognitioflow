# Week 6 — Land Law: Transfer and Registration

*Schmid & Hertel, EUI/DNotI General Report (2005), Ch 1–4 · Ploeger, van Velten & Zevenbergen, Netherlands country report (2005) · Ekbäck (2009) · Engine E7*

> Gate (E7): name the register type before analysing third-party effect — on three axes: (1) what is registered (rights / realfolium vs documents / personalfolium); (2) constitutive vs declaratory effect; (3) whether good faith in the register is protected (positive vs negative). The DCFR has no land law; this week runs on national systems.

## General Report — Chapter 1: principles

Common Roman/feudal roots. Unitary civilian *dominium* vs common-law **estates** (time-limited ownership; freehold as functional equivalent) and trusts/equitable interests. *Superficies solo cedit* is universal, with building leases and apartment ownership as the standard exceptions (§ 912 BGB *Überbau* noted). **Numerus clausus** — rigid in FR/DE/IT/PT/GR, "softer" in NL/Scotland/Finland (statutory cap but contractual shaping), **not recognised in England** (courts created proprietary estoppel despite LPA 1925 s. 4(1)); its ratio is that *erga omnes* enforceability requires third-party foreseeability. Limited rights are classified as rights of use (usufruct, habitation, emphyteusis, superficies, servitudes), security rights (accessory in the FR/English families; non-accessory *Grundschuld* in the German family) and pre-emption rights.

## General Report — Chapter 2: the typology of registers

Five basic types emerge from the three axes: the **British Isles** system ("registration is the right" — LRA 2002 s. 7(1): disposition void as to the legal estate if not registered; s. 23(1): owner's powers vest in whoever is registered; s. 65 & Sched. 4: proprietor in possession protected against rectification); the **Central-European Grundbuch** (constitutive + public faith, court-kept — § 873 BGB: agreement plus registration; § 925 *Auflassung*; § 311b notarial contract); the **Nordic** register (declaratory but good faith protected); the **French** *conservation des hypothèques* (documents, declaratory, no good-faith protection — décret 4 Jan 1955 art. 30: unregistered acts *inopposable* to third parties with registered competing rights; art. 2200 CC order of filing); and the old common-law deeds registry. The **Netherlands is a hybrid**: constitutive registration but only "some" good-faith protection, traditionally called a **negative** system. **Rank** of limited rights follows time of registration everywhere (the A/B/C mortgage case: registered B, C, A → rank B–C–A). **Priority notices** bridge the gap between contract and registration: German *Vormerkung* (§ 883 BGB, no time limit, requires a valid obligation); NL art. 7:3 BW (six months); England's official-search priority period; Austria § 53 GBG (one year); Italy art. 2645-bis CC.

## General Report — Chapter 3: sale of real estate (titulus/modus)

Causal vs abstract on one axis, declaratory vs constitutive on the other: **only Germany and the British Isles are abstract**; all abstract systems are constitutive and all declaratory systems are causal, but **causal-and-constitutive exists (Austria, NL)**. Payment is never a statutory requirement of transfer. Title search is needed in France (whole chain, ~30 years) but not in Germany; still done in NL practice. Forms: authentic act for registration (FR); notarial contract and Auflassung (DE); notarial deed of conveyance (NL); signed, witnessed deed (England). Risk: § 446 BGB passes risk on transfer of possession; *Kauf bricht nicht Miete* § 566 BGB.

## General Report — Chapter 4: the three cases, party by party

- **(a) Invalid A–B contract, sale on to C:** causal systems (FR, IT, NL) — B never owner, C cannot acquire (and once C knows, no good faith anyway); abstract systems (DE, Finland) — formal invalidity does not hit the transfer, incapacity does; England — C protected if in good faith unless A is in possession.

- **(b) Registered seller is not the owner:** B acquires in DE, PL, PT, ES; in England unless the true owner is in occupation; **not in BE, FR, IT, NL** — there only prescription over time cures (10 years Italy, 30 years France).

- **(c) Creditor distrains after contract, before registration:** the decisive factor is a priority notice or register freeze, not the constitutive/declaratory label.

## Netherlands country report — the NL cells

- **History and concept.** Napoleonic base with a Germanic graft: **constitutive registration** of the deed (Code of 1838, carried into the 1992 BW). Ownership is unitary; no "estate"; apartment ownership follows the *système unitaire*.

- **Superficies.** Art. 5:20 BW; exceptions: horizontal accession (HR 28 Oct 1994, *Makkee/De Werdt*), the real right of *opstal* (art. 5:101 BW) and statutes. *Erfpacht* (art. 5:85) does **not** break vertical accession — erfpacht ≠ opstal.

- **Numerus clausus for land.** Eigendom, erfpacht, opstal, vruchtgebruik, gebruik en bewoning, appartementsrecht, mandeligheid, erfdienstbaarheden, hypotheek; few mandatory rules, so content is largely shaped by contract. Only appurtenant servitudes exist (arts 5:70–5:71 BW — the report misprints "3:70/3:71"); the personal-servitude gap is filled by the contractual *kwalitatieve verplichting* (art. 6:252 BW), which binds successors after registration but is **not a real right**.

- **Registration.** Art. 3:16 BW plus the Kadasterwet; the Kadaster combines cadastre and public registers; almost all registrable documents are notarial deeds; the registrar checks formalities only, not the seller's title (art. 19 Kw) — an "inactive" registrar. **Constitutive** (arts 3:84 jo 3:89 BW) but **"negative"**: registration does not cure substantive defects; third-party protection under **arts 3:24–3:26 BW** is partial — you may assume that what is *not* registered does not exist, but you cannot rely blindly on what *is* registered; **art. 3:88 BW** protects a buyer only where the seller's lack of power stems from an *invalid earlier transfer*, not where an earlier transferor simply lacked the power to dispose. Art. 3:22 cures formal, not material, defects. Rank follows time of filing (art. 3:19(2)); the priority notice (**art. 7:3 BW**, since 2003) shields the buyer for six months against later alienations, encumbrances, attachments and the seller's bankruptcy. State liability where a third-party-protection rule costs an owner his right (art. 3:30). Deeds of conveyance only by a Dutch notary (art. 3:31).

- **Transfer.** Causal; three requirements — valid title, real agreement implied in the deed, registration (arts 3:84, 3:89 BW; the deed must state the title, 3:89(2)); payment is not a requirement but the notary's escrow (art. 7:26(3) BW) synchronises it; consumer house-sales must be in writing with a three-day cooling-off (art. 7:2 BW); seller must deliver free of undisclosed registrable encumbrances (art. 7:15) and the property must be fit for normal use (art. 7:17); title search theoretically back over the prescription period (20 years; 10 in good faith).

- **Mortgage.** Single accessory *hypotheek* (art. 3:227), created by notarial deed plus registration stating claim and maximum amount (art. 3:260); credit/bank mortgage for future claims (3:231); *rangregeling* by consent (3:262); direct enforcement by public sale before a notary without court order (3:268); ranks before privileges (3:279); passes with the claim (3:94); ends by merger (3:81(2)(e)).

- **Cases.** HR 28 Oct 1994, NJ 1995, 96 (*Makkee/De Werdt* — horizontal accession); HR 23 Feb 1994 (windmills) and HR 31 Oct 1997 (portocabin) — movable/immovable by objectively apparent permanent attachment; HR 10 Mar 1995 (*Ameland State*; *Duinroos-Duindistel*); HR 24 May 2002 (*Winkelhof*).

## Ekbäck — property rights in land, economically

Law-and-economics article on Swedish institutions. Private exclusive rights internalise externalities (Demsetz; Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" actually describes **open access**, not true commons), but creating and enforcing them carries **institutional costs** (surveying, registers, boundary marking, exclusion, monitoring), so complete privatisation is never efficient at the margin; some uses show **economies of scale** (roads, water, drainage) where common property saves transaction costs. Ownership in Sweden is *negatively* determined (all powers not removed by law); limited rights are *positively* defined by contract or statute — a neat map onto numerus clausus. Transferable content: ownership as an economic "bundle of rights" (use, exclude, transfer, take value); the three regimes; why a cadastre/register exists in economic terms. **Trap:** the economic bundle picture is not the civil-law unitary concept (VIII.-1:202; art. 5:1 BW; art. 544 CC).

## Exam hooks and traps

- "Classify the Dutch land register: positive or negative?" → name the three axes; NL = constitutive + limited good-faith protection = "negative". **WRONG_TOOL:** constitutive ⇒ positive (NL and Greece break the correlation).

- "A (registered) sells to B in good faith; A was never owner." → NL: B **not** protected (art. 3:88 cures only an invalid earlier transfer); DE: protected; England: protected unless the true owner is in occupation. **GATE_SKIP:** describing the register instead of saying whether B is owner.

- "Is England's system good-faith protection?" → **PRECISION:** it is the s. 23(1) power of disposition, not bona fides; possession protects against rectification.

- "Mortgages to A, then B, then C; registered B, C, A — rank?" → B–C–A (art. 3:19(2) BW); rangregeling (3:262) reorders.

- "Is the kwalitatieve verplichting a limited real right?" → No — registered contract with successor-binding effect.

- "What protects the buyer between signing and registration?" → art. 7:3 priority notice; notarial escrow (7:26(3)); causal system.

- "Why title search in France but not Germany?" → good-faith protection in the register.

- "Explain causal/abstract and constitutive/declaratory with one system per combination" → never collapse the axes (E4).
