## Sagaert — Consensual versus delivery systems (Faber/Lurger, pp. 9–46)

Three possible moments (consent, delivery, payment); no European system adopts payment. Roman law converged on **delivery** (*traditionibus … dominia rerum, non nudis pactis transferuntur*); French practice eroded this through the *clause de dessaisine-saisine*, which the 1804 Code presumed into every contract — so the French "consensual" system is historically a **fictitious delivery system**. Consensual systems (FR, BE, IT, LUX; SGA ss. 17–18) transfer by the obligation itself, have no *Trennungsprinzip*, and are therefore **never abstract**. But the rule is riddled with exceptions: third-party effect requires delivery (art. 1141 CC — double sale: first bona fide possessor wins; now art. 1198); generic and future goods need individualisation (art. 1585 CC; SGA ss. 16, 20A–20B); gifts from hand to hand require dispossession; retention of title ("ownership as queen of security rights", art. 2367 CC) means ownership in fact rarely passes at consent. Delivery systems (DE § 929; NL art. 3:84; AT § 425 ABGB; CH art. 714 ZGB) separate obligatory and real agreement; **all abstract systems are delivery systems, but causal systems may be either.** Delivery is softened by *traditio ficta* (brevi manu § 929(2); constitutum possessorium § 930; longa manu § 931), and constructive possessors get weaker protection (§ 933 BGB). **Conclusion:** the consensual/delivery divide is "more a matter of concept than of result" — far less substantial than causal/abstract. Harmonisation should be **functional**: risk (an obligations question — notice returns risk to the seller; *genera non pereunt*), third-party effects, insolvency of the transferor (delivery systems: buyer unprotected; consensual: protected in principle) and of the transferee (right of retention; stoppage in transitu L624-13 / SGA s. 44; unpaid seller's lien SGA s. 41).

**Cases:** Cass fr 30 June 1925 and 31 Oct 1955 (individualisation, no retroactivity); Cass fr 1 Aug 1950 (future goods pass when deliverable); Belgian Cass 9 May 1947 (revindication only of specific goods); BGH 13 Jan 1992, NJW 1992, 1161 (real agreement needs specified goods).

## Bartels — An abstract or a causal system (Faber/Lurger, pp. 59–67)

Prefers a **result-based** approach ("can a defrauded seller reclaim from the bankrupt buyer?"). **Abstract** systems (DE, Greece, Scotland, South Africa, Estonia): the transferee stays owner despite a void or avoided legal basis; the transferor has a personal restitution claim and ranks as an **unsecured creditor** in the transferee's insolvency. **Causal** systems: invalidity of the obligation invalidates the transfer, ownership reverts retroactively, the seller **revindicates** from the estate. The abstract system's real argument is protection of trade and third parties; the causal system rests on not divesting an owner without good reason — at the price of legal certainty. In practice both converge: causal systems add far-reaching bona fide protection; German law makes the real agreement voidable for fraud/duress (*Fehleridentität*). The difference stays prominent for **creditors**. Causal systems differ among themselves: the Dutch *causa* shifted from "obligation to transfer" to Hartkamp's "legal relationship that justifies the transfer" (*Van Wessem/Traffic* HR 1970; *Breezand/Veere* HR 2000; art. 6:211(2) BW as exception); and in NL since 1992 **termination for breach** has no retroactive proprietary effect — "comparable with the results of an abstract system". Van Vliet's **animus theory** (transfer follows the genuine will to transfer, not contract validity) is a midway. Conclusion: the choice is a starting point, not a matter of principle.

## Van Vliet — Book VIII DCFR (ZEuP 2011) — Parts I–II

The DCFR chose a **unitary** approach (one moment) over the Nordic functional approach, and a **causal delivery system**: delivery by default, but parties may agree another moment (VIII.-2:101(1)(e)) — a flexibility resembling SGA s. 17, though the SGA's *default* is consensual, so classify each by its default. The causal character appears in 2:101(1)(d), (2) and 2:202; revindication after an invalid ground under 6:102. Van Vliet's main critique: the drafters **rejected the real agreement**, so transfer is not treated as a juridical act — inconsistent with II.-1:101(2), with 2:203 (conditions attached to a transfer), with Book X and with assignment being a juridical act. He walks through indirect representation (2:302, cf. art. 3:110 BW), the "agreement as to time" (which is *not* a transfer of possession, so even stolen goods can be transferred this way), chain transactions (2:303), the SGA-inspired **bulk co-ownership** regime (2:101(3), 2:305–2:306; loss falls first on the seller's share) and the novel **double-sale** rule (2:301). **Good-faith acquisition (VIII.-3:101)** requires all other transfer requirements, **actual delivery** (so no protection via constitutum possessorium — the DCFR follows DE § 933, FR arts 1141/2276, NL, contra SGA s. 24 and *Gerson v Wilkinson*), **value**, strict **good faith** (ordinary negligence disqualifies) with the **burden of proof on the acquirer**; exceptions for stolen goods (unless sold in the ordinary course of business) and cultural objects (VIII.-4:102). He criticises the odd treatment of resolutive conditions (2:203 applies nemo dat to the *re*-transfer, so a third party is protected even in bad faith) and argues the physical-control requirement should be dropped, especially since IX.-2:108 protects good-faith acquirers of non-possessory security rights.

**Cases:** *Gerson (Leasing) v Wilkinson* [2001] QB 514 — SGA s. 24 protects a buyer receiving constructive delivery; Cass 6 June 1886 — French good-faith acquisition is original, no valid contract needed; HR 18 Sept 1987 (*Berg/De Bary*).

## Salomons — A tale of two gatekeepers (ERPL 2013)

Good-faith acquisition (GFA) and acquisitive prescription (AP) are **subsidiary modes of acquisition** that step in when a transfer fails: first test GFA; if it fails, AP is the "last and final hope" — the **two gatekeepers of ownership**. Historically: Roman law = "GFA no, AP yes"; Code civil 1804 (art. 2279, now 2276, *possession vaut titre* — GFA dressed as *prescription instantanée*) = "AP no, GFA yes"; modern codes (BGB, BW 1992) = both. Since GFA acquired conditions (good faith, value, delivery, valid causa), gaps appear (donee, negligent acquirer, avoided contract) that only AP can close. **VIII.-3:101** vs **VIII.-4:101** — three differences in the good-faith element: **content** (transferor's title vs one's own title), **duration** (*mala fides superveniens non nocet* vs throughout), **burden** (on acquirer vs presumed). Policy: GFA serves **commerce**; AP serves **legal certainty** more broadly. Critique: several GFA conditions (strict negligence standard, burden of proof, exclusion of constructive delivery) are likely *detrimental* to commerce, pushing acquirers onto AP — but AP's **10-year period** (3 years is the European norm) and good faith throughout make AP nearly unreachable for mass-produced movables. Result: functionally still "GFA yes, AP no" — incoherent gatekeeping.

**Cases:** HR 29 April 1928 and 10 May 1929 (bad-faith acquirers unprotected); HR 2 Dec 1994, NJ 1995, 746 (acquisition for value); HR 29 June 1855 (GFA as "prescription after zero years").

## Exam hooks and traps

- "A sells a horse to B — owner immediately? Two answers" → consensual vs tradition (WG Week 1 Q12).

- "Contract void/avoided; buyer insolvent — can the seller revindicate?" → causal vs abstract; the insolvency stress test.

- "Non-owner sells with constitutum possessorium to bona fide C" → VIII.-3:101(1)(b): **not** protected; compare § 933 BGB, art. 1198 CC, SGA s. 24.

- "S sells twice; delivers to B2 who knew of B1" → VIII.-2:301.

- "What are the two gatekeepers and how do they differ?" → the grid table.

- **Traps:** collapsing the two axes; assuming good faith is presumed under VIII.-3:101 (it is not); forgetting the ordinary-course-of-business exception for stolen goods; treating termination as avoidance (2:202(3)); saying the seller-of-money is protected (specificity defeats it); labelling "abstract" as common law.
