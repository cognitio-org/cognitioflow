# Week 1 — Working Group Brief (Q1–Q12)

*Introduction to property law: property vs personal rights, principles, ownership & possession*

> Instruction on the sheet: "Please always justify your answer. Also, please refer to, and apply, applicable norms and principles." — no bare assertions. Every answer names a principle or an article.
> FRAMEWORK — GOVERNING TEST FOR THE WEEK
> Every Week 1 question resolves through one divide and five principles. State the divide, then reach for the principle that does the work.
> THE DIVIDE (E1). A property right is absolute — it works erga omnes, against the world. A personal right is relative — it works inter partes, only between the parties to the obligation.
> WHAT "ABSOLUTE" DOES NOT MEAN. It does not mean the holder may do whatever they like with the thing. Absolute effect is a relational concept: the whole world must respect the right. Limited rights are absolute in exactly this sense too — not only ownership.
> THE HALLMARKS that follow from absoluteness: (1) Droit de suite — the right sticks to the object. (2) Droit de préférence — the holder ranks ahead of unsecured creditors and survives insolvency. (3) Prior tempore potior iure — between competing property rights the earlier right wins; scope: only where several limited rights of different persons burden the same object and conflict.
> THE PRINCIPLES: (4) Numerus clausus — closed list (Typenzwang + Typenfixierung). (5) Specificity — a property right exists only in a specific, identified object. (6) Publicity — the world must be able to discover the right (delivery for movables; deed + register for immovables). (7) Nemo dat quod non habet / nemo plus iuris.
> PRINCIPLES ARE PRINCIPLES. Rules carve exceptions: droit de suite yields to good-faith third-party protection; nemo plus yields to good-faith acquisition (Week 3). State the principle, then the rule-level exception.
> THE CLOSING MOVE. Every answer ends by saying against whom the right can be exerted.

## Case 1 — Doctrinal foundations

> 🎙 🎙 FROM THE SEMINAR. "Absolute" is a relational concept — Verstijlen pressed this twice. Limited rights are absolute too: the bank's right of pledge over Lisa's car binds the world exactly as her ownership does — two parties hold property rights in the same object at the same time. The cake picture: ownership is the whole cake; the limited right is a slice cut out of it. Why droit de suite proves it: if Lisa sells the pledged car, the buyer takes ownership but the pledge rides along.

### Q1. What are the characteristics of property rights as opposed to personal rights?

**Issue.** By what criterion is a right classified as proprietary rather than obligational, and what follows?

**Rule.** Criterion: a property right is **absolute** (*erga omnes*); a personal right is **relative** (*inter partes*). Consequences: **droit de suite**, **droit de préférence** (see Q8), **prior tempore potior iure** (see Q11). Not confined to ownership — limited rights carry absolute effect too. Structural features: **numerus clausus**, **specificity**, **publicity**; personal rights have none of these. Source of the difference: property rights derive from the **law itself**, not party consent — a person bound by a right they never agreed to must be able to discover it.

**Application.** Classification is the first move. A right *not on the closed list* is not a property right, however the parties describe it — it takes effect as a contractual right with relative effect only.

**Conclusion.** Property rights are absolute, bind third parties, follow the object, enjoy priority in insolvency, and exist only in the closed and public forms the law provides. Personal rights are relative, bind only the counterparty, and are freely designed but confer no advantage against third parties or in insolvency.

> 🎙 🎙 Lecture hook — the gas-station example. A contractual right to fill the tank free is valid but not on the closed list. If the seller sells the station to X, X can turn the buyer away. Off-list rights get relative effect only.
> 🎙 Noorlander v Ligtvoet shape. Contractual right against the original owner; land passes to a successor who knew. Knowledge does not bind.

### Q2. What is property law about?

**Rule.** Property law governs the **relationship between persons and things, and the effect of those relations on third parties.** Three strands: (1) **which rights** may exist (numerus clausus); (2) **how** they are created, transferred, encumbered and lost; (3) **against whom** they can be exerted. Consequence: property law carries **more formalities** than contract and tort — formality is the price of binding strangers.

**Application.** Property law is **the law of allocation of things among competing claimants** — which is why it is largely **mandatory law**. The third-party dimension explains every peculiarity of the field.

**Conclusion.** Property law is about the relation between persons and things and, decisively, the effect of that relation on third parties.

> ⚠ ⚠ Do not answer "property law is about ownership." Ownership is one right within the field, not the field.

### Q3. Movable vs immovable property — and the purpose of the distinction?

**Rule.** **Immovables:** land and what is permanently attached to it (*superficies solo cedit*). **Movables:** corporeal things capable of being moved. **VIII.–1:201 DCFR** defines *"goods"* as **corporeal movables**, expressly including ships, aircraft, animals, liquids and gases. Book VIII governs movables only; **VIII.–1:102** leaves registration of goods to **national law**. (NL: art. 3:3 BW defines immovables/movables — verified in the national provisions file.)

**Application.** The purpose is **publicity**, scaled to the asset: **immovables** are unique, fixed and **cannot be hidden** — a **public land register** works; **movables** are numerous, mobile, easily concealed — publicity via the cruder proxy of **possession and delivery**. Consequences: different transfer requirements, third-party protection, and security devices (mortgage/hypothec vs pledge).

**Conclusion.** The two classes require **different publicity mechanisms** and therefore different transfer and protection rules.

> ⚠ ⚠ Watch out — the rationale inverts easily. Immovables cannot be hidden — that is exactly why registration works for them.

### Q4. What is ownership? Is there a different notion in common law jurisdictions?

**(i) DCFR / civilian.** **VIII.–1:202**: the **most comprehensive right** a person can have over property, including the **exclusive** right — so far as consistent with law or rights granted by the owner — to **use, enjoy, modify, destroy, dispose of and recover** it. **Do not truncate the list** — *recover* (the *rei vindicatio*) is the enforcement limb. Ownership is **unitary and abstract** (one indivisible right; co-ownership in undivided shares, **VIII.–1:203**) and **elastic** (limited rights diminish it temporarily; it springs back). Compare **Art. 544 Code civil** (enjoy and dispose in the most absolute manner, subject to law — verified) and **art. 5:1 BW** (most comprehensive right — verified).

**(ii) Common law.** No unitary, abstract ownership; the organising idea is **title**, which is **relative** and **fragmented**. *Armory v Delamirie* (1722) — a finder has title good against everyone except the true owner. **Estates in land** (freehold, leasehold) layer interests in time. **Law and equity** split legal and equitable title (trustee/beneficiary) — hence the DCFR's separate **Book X** on trusts.

**Conclusion.** Materially different: a single comprehensive right vs relative, fragmented **title** and **estates**.

### Q5. Ownership vs possession in civil law?

**Rule.** **Ownership = a RIGHT** (**VIII.–1:202**). **Possession = a FACT** — **VIII.–1:205**: direct or indirect **physical control**, a factual situation the law protects. The intention element sorts possessors: **VIII.–1:206 owner-possessor** (as, or as if, an owner); **VIII.–1:207 limited-right-possessor**; **VIII.–1:208 possession-agent**. NL: **art. 3:107(1) BW** — possession is holding a thing for oneself (cited in Hoops's reading).

**Application.** The two come apart in both directions: a **thief possesses but does not own**; a **landlord owns but does not possess**. The law protects possession anyway: (a) evidentiary presumption; (b) public order (no self-help); (c) **publicity** for movables. Possession can ripen into ownership through **time** via **acquisitive prescription** (Book VIII Ch. 4) — not through intention alone.

**Conclusion.** Ownership is a **right**; possession is a **fact** qualified by intention. Either can exist without the other.

> ⚠ ⚠ WRONG_TOOL: possession is not "the right to enjoy the thing for a limited period" — that is a limited right. ⚠ PRECISION: intention distinguishes an owner-possessor from a limited-right-possessor; time converts possession into ownership.

### Q6. Numerus clausus — what and why?

**Rule.** Property rights form a **closed list**; the types are **fixed by law**; parties **cannot create new ones**. Two limbs: **Typenzwang** (*which* rights — the catalogue differs between systems) and **Typenfixierung** (*what content* — a usufruct is use + fruits; extras are merely obligational). **Controlled flexibility:** servitudes can be shaped within the type.

**Purpose.** (1) **Third-party transparency** — these rights bind non-consenting persons who must know what they are up against. (2) **Legal certainty and low information costs** — a finite catalogue of encumbrances. (3) **Protecting the integrity of ownership** against idiosyncratic fragmentation. (4) **Institutional legitimacy** — creation is for the legislature, not private agreement.

**Conclusion.** Property rights exist only in the types and with the content laid down by law; purpose = protection of third parties.

### Q7. What does the specificity principle entail?

**Rule.** A property right can only exist in a **specific, individually identified object**. **VIII.–2:101(3)**: where goods are defined in **generic terms**, ownership passes only when they are **identified**; goods in an identified bulk → **VIII.–2:305**. Rationale: *"the very nature of things"* (Lord Blackburn). Corollary: an aggregate (a business, a herd) is not itself an object of a property right.

**Application.** A buyer of "100 tonnes of wheat" acquires **no property right** until identification and therefore has **only a personal claim** — which fails in the seller's insolvency (Q8). Ordering copies from a bookshop: no property right until specific copies are appropriated; prepayment does not change this. *Re Goldcorp Exchange* [1995]: buyers of unallocated bullion ranked as unsecured creditors. Mixed money cannot be revindicated — the payer falls back on unjustified enrichment (van Vliet).

> ⚠ ⚠ The most-confused pair of the week. Specificity = rights in specific objects. Numerus clausus = a closed list of rights. One is about the object, the other about the right.

### Q8. The right of separation — A transfers a car to B but holds it for a week; A turns insolvent. Must the trustee return the car?

**Issue.** Does B's entitlement survive A's insolvency, or does B rank as an ordinary creditor?

**Rule.** The **right of separation** (German *Aussonderungsrecht*) is the right of an **owner** to have a thing **separated out of** the insolvent estate, because it never belonged to the debtor. This is **droit de préférence** in its purest form: a property right survives the counterparty's insolvency; a personal right does not. Did ownership pass though A kept the car? Yes: **VIII.–2:101(1)(e)** — ownership passes where there is **an agreement as to the time ownership is to pass**, or absent that, delivery or an equivalent; **VIII.–2:103** confirms parties may fix the moment. This is ***constitutum possessorium***: the transferor retains physical control, now holding for the new owner (**VIII.–1:207/1:208**; B possesses **indirectly**, **VIII.–1:205**).

**Application.** Run **VIII.–2:101(1)** clause by clause: car **exists** (a); **transferable** (b); **A had the right to transfer** (c); **B entitled as against A** by contract (d); **agreement as to time** taken effect (e). **B is owner** when insolvency strikes. B's right is **absolute** — against A's **creditors and trustee**.

**Conclusion.** **Yes — the trustee must hand the car over to B.** Had ownership *not* yet passed (retention-of-ownership clause; generic unidentified goods), B would have held only a **personal claim to delivery** and ranked as an **unsecured creditor**.

> Why this question exists. Insolvency is the stress test that reveals the property/personal divide as the difference between full recovery and a few cents in the euro. Van Vliet: in a causal system ownership reverts automatically on avoidance and the seller can revindicate; in an abstract system the seller holds only a personal claim in unjustified enrichment. Both are civil-law positions — FR and NL causal; DE abstract; the common law is not on this axis.
> ⚠ Van Vliet's caveat: protection via constitutum possessorium is imperfect — A retains control and may pass the car to a good-faith acquirer; and an acquirer who himself acquired by constitutum possessorium cannot invoke VIII.–3:101, which requires delivery or an equivalent.

## Case 2 — The house, the mortgage and the occupant (Q9–Q11)

> METHOD (E0) — run this before writing anything. (1) Fix the timeline of juridical acts. (2) For each party: what right, property or personal, how acquired, against whom? (3) Resolve every conflict with prior tempore and third-party-protection rules. (4) Conclude for each named party separately.

### Q9. A buys a house from B for €150,000; transferred to A. A borrows from bank C and registers a mortgage for C. A lets D live in the house. Discuss the rights and obligations of A, B, C and D.

*Timeline: (1) B → A sale and transfer. (2) mortgage registered for C. (3) A permits D to occupy.*

**Rule.** Transfer of immovables requires a **notarial deed entered in the public register** (publicity); registration is **constitutive** in NL (art. 3:89 BW) and DE (§ 873 BGB). A **mortgage/hypothec** is a **limited proprietary security right** carved out of A's ownership. **Numerus clausus** decides whether D's entitlement can be proprietary at all.

**A — the owner.** Right: ownership, **absolute**, *erga omnes*, **encumbered** by C's mortgage — and elastic. ⚠ Keep A's two columns apart: what A *owes* is **personal** — repayment to C (loan contract, separate from the mortgage), the occupancy arrangement with D, the price to B.

**B — the seller.** Having transferred ownership, B retains **no property right**. B holds a **personal claim** for the price — **relative**, against A only. If A becomes insolvent, B is an **ordinary unsecured creditor** (contrast Q8). Had B taken a mortgage for the price, B would have a proprietary position.

**C — the bank.** **Two distinct rights:** the **claim to repayment** — **personal**, against A; the **right of mortgage** — a **limited property right**, **absolute**, with **droit de suite** (Q10), **droit de préférence**, and the right to **foreclose and sell** on default.

**D — the occupant.** The party the question is really testing. A lease or gratuitous permission is created by **contract** — **relative**, against A only: no proprietary act was performed, and a mere permission to occupy is **not on the closed list**. Had A created a **usufruct** or **right of habitation** by notarial deed + registration, D would hold a **limited property right** against the world. ⚠ Nuance: Dutch law softens this by statute — *koop breekt geen huur* (sale does not break hire) — a **statutory exception giving third-party effect to a contractual right**; it does **not** convert the lease into a property right. *(Verify the provision — believed art. 7:226 BW — not in the national provisions file.)* Compare § 566 BGB (*Kauf bricht nicht Miete*), cited in the General Report.

**Conclusion.** **A**: ownership, absolute, encumbered; personal obligations to B, C, D. **B**: personal claim, against A only. **C**: mortgage (absolute) + personal repayment claim (against A). **D**: personal right of occupation, against A only, vulnerable to a sale and to C's enforcement.

### Q10. What is C's position if A sells and transfers the house to E after registration of the mortgage?

**Rule.** **Droit de suite** — a limited property right follows the object into a successor's hands. **Nemo plus iuris** — A held ownership encumbered by C's mortgage and can transfer only encumbered ownership. **Publicity** — the mortgage was registered; E could and should have consulted the register, so **no third-party protection**. **Prior tempore** — C's right predates E's acquisition.

**Application.** C's mortgage survives **unimpaired**; E acquires **subject to** it. C's two rights now sit against two different people: the **mortgage** burdens **E's** house (C may foreclose although E never borrowed a cent); the **repayment claim** remains **personal and against A**. E is not personally liable (absent assumption of debt). E's recourse is a **personal** claim against **A**.

**Conclusion.** C's position is **materially unaffected**. Security against E's house; personal claim against A.

> Q9 and Q10 are the same lesson told twice: D (personal right) is swept aside when the house changes hands; C (property right) is untouched. Keep the debt and the security rigorously apart — conflating them is a PRECISION error.

### Q11. A registers a mortgage for Bank B, then a usufruct for C. A defaults; B enforces. Effect on C's right?

**Rule.** ***Prior tempore potior iure*** — B's mortgage was registered **first**. ***Nemo plus iuris*** — A could only create a usufruct **subject to** the mortgage. A remained owner with power to dispose, so the usufruct was **valid** — it simply ranks **behind** B. **Enforcement clears junior rights**: the senior mortgagee may sell **free of** later limited rights to realise **full value** (NL: *zuiveringsrecht*). Were it otherwise, A could defeat any mortgage by granting a lifelong usufruct the day after registration.

**Application.** In peacetime the rights coexist. On default, B forecloses and sells **without** the usufruct; **C's usufruct is extinguished**; the purchaser takes unencumbered. C is left with: a claim on **surplus proceeds**; a **personal claim against A**; **nothing against B or the purchaser**.

**Conclusion.** **C's usufruct is extinguished.** C is relegated to the surplus and a personal claim against A.

> ⚠ ⚠ Compare with Q10 — the examinable contrast. Q10: C's mortgage survives a transfer (droit de suite against a later acquirer). Q11: C's usufruct is destroyed by enforcement of an earlier right (prior tempore). A property right follows the thing against successors but yields to a right that came first. Counterfactual: had the usufruct been registered before the mortgage, B would have taken its security subject to it and lent against a lower value.

### Q12. A sells a horse to B. Does B become owner immediately? Two different answers — explain.

> THE GATE. The two answers exist because systems differ on one axis: consent alone vs delivery. Classify the system before answering. ⚠ Do not confuse this with the causal/abstract axis — they are orthogonal.

**(i) Consensual system — YES.** Ownership passes ***solo consensu*** at the moment of contract; delivery is merely performance. **France**: **Art. 1196 Code civil** — in contracts to alienate property, transfer takes place at conclusion of the contract *(verified in the national provisions file)*. English law under the **SGA 1979 ss. 17–18** is consensual-leaning: property in specific goods passes **when the parties intend**. The horse is **specific**, so specificity is satisfied at contract. B bears the **risk** and is protected as owner in **A's insolvency**.

**(ii) Tradition system — NO, not until delivery.** The contract creates only an **obligation to transfer**; ownership passes on **delivery** or an equivalent. **Germany** (§ 929 BGB), the **Netherlands** (art. 3:84 BW) and the **DCFR**. **VIII.–2:101(1)**: (a) goods exist; (b) transferable; (c) transferor's right or authority; (d) transferee entitled as against transferor; (e) agreement as to time **or, absent that, delivery or an equivalent**. **VIII.–2:104** — delivery = transferor gives up and transferee obtains possession. **VIII.–2:105** — equivalents: goods already in transferee's possession; notice to a third-party holder; handing over the **means** of control (keys); transfer of a document of title. Here (a)–(d) are met; the **hinge is (e)**: no delivery and no timing agreement → **ownership has not passed**; B has a **personal claim** and ranks as an **unsecured creditor**. **The DCFR's opt-out:** **VIII.–2:101(1)(e)** + **VIII.–2:103** let the parties **agree** the moment — earlier (*constitutum possessorium*) or later (retention of ownership, **VIII.–2:307**).

**Further points worth marks.** **Generic goods:** "a horse" rather than *this* horse → **VIII.–2:101(3)** bars passing until identification; even consensual systems require identification. **Why the divergence matters:** risk, insolvency protection (Q8), and who can transfer to a third party. **The second axis, if invited:** **causal** (FR, NL, DCFR) — a void *causa* kills the transfer; **abstract** (DE) — the real agreement is independent. **Germany is abstract *and* tradition; France is causal *and* consensual; the Netherlands is causal *and* tradition** — which proves the axes are independent.

## Point of care — the traps this week

| Confuse              | With                   | Keep straight by                                                                                     |
| -------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Numerus clausus      | Specificity            | Closed list of rights vs rights in specific objects                                                  |
| Ownership            | Possession             | A right vs a fact (physical control + intention)                                                     |
| Possession           | Limited right          | Possession is not "ownership for a limited time" — that is a usufruct/lease                          |
| Nemo dat             | Nemo plus              | Cannot transfer what you don't have vs cannot grant more than you hold                               |
| Priority principle   | Nemo plus              | Two limited rights on one object (rank by date) vs transferor had nothing left to give (double sale) |
| Causal / abstract    | Civil law / common law | Both are civilian — FR/NL causal, DE abstract; the common law is on neither                          |
| A's right            | A's obligations        | Ownership of the house (property, absolute) vs what A owes B, C and D (personal)                     |
| Absolute effect      | Freedom of use         | The world must respect the right — not that the holder may do as they like                           |
| Consensual/tradition | Causal/abstract        | When ownership passes vs whether a void contract defeats it — orthogonal axes                        |
| The debt             | The security           | Personal, stays with the debtor vs proprietary, follows the thing (Q10)                              |
| Droit de suite       | Prior tempore          | Survives later acquirers (Q10) vs yields to earlier rights (Q11)                                     |

> 🎙 🎙 FROM THE SEMINAR — scope limit on the priority principle. Prior tempore potior iure applies only where several limited rights, held by different persons, burden the same object and conflict (Sam and the bank each hold a pledge over Lisa's car — the older pledge prevails, because it already bound the whole world, including Sam). The trap — a double sale is not a priority case. Owner transfers a laptop to A, then purports to sell and deliver it to B: that is nemo plus iuris — the seller had nothing left to give. If no dates are given for two competing pledges, the correct answer is insufficient information to rank them — say so rather than guessing.

**Two habits that earn marks:** (1) **Define before applying.** (2) **Always say against whom.**
